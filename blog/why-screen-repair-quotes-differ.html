Why the same screen repair is quoted differently by two shops on the same street

When you walk into two different repair shops on the same street with a cracked screen, you might expect identical quotes. Instead, you often receive two vastly different numbers. It is easy to assume that the shop charging more is simply trying to inflate their margins, or that the shop charging less is cutting corners. Neither assumption tells the full story. The difference in quotes is usually a reflection of the specific parts, labour standards, and business models each shop operates under. Understanding these factors can help you make an informed decision when you decide whether to pay for a repair.

The hierarchy of replacement parts

The single biggest factor in the cost of a screen repair is the origin and quality of the replacement glass. Manufacturers generally do not sell official spare parts to independent repairers, which forces a tiered market of alternatives. At the top of this hierarchy are genuine components salvaged from other devices. These parts are identical to what was on your phone when it left the factory, but because they are salvaged, their supply is inconsistent and relies on professional extraction processes.

Below salvaged parts are 'soft OLED' or 'hard OLED' aftermarket screens. Soft OLEDs are designed to match the flexibility and colour profile of your original display, whereas hard OLEDs are more rigid and sometimes slightly thicker, which can make them more susceptible to cracking if the phone is dropped again. At the bottom of the quality tier are generic LCD replacements for phones that originally used OLED technology. These cheaper parts often result in lower screen brightness, altered colour saturation, and higher battery consumption. A shop using a high-quality salvaged part will inevitably quote higher than a shop using a budget LCD panel, even if the work required to install them is exactly the same.

Labour, expertise, and operational overheads

Repairing a modern smartphone is not just about unscrewing a back panel; it requires precision tools, microscope inspection, and an understanding of microscopic board components. The level of experience of the technician carries a premium. An established shop with certified technicians who have years of experience with complex board-level repairs will often charge more for their labour than a store that only performs basic component swaps.

Operational overheads also play a significant role. A shop that offers a comprehensive warranty on their work, maintains a physical storefront with staff to handle customer inquiries, and invests in high-end anti-static workbenches will have higher running costs than a 'back-pocket' operator working out of a home office. When you pay for a repair, you are often paying for the peace of mind that comes with a legitimate business that will still be there if you need to bring the phone back for a warranty claim. Inventory management and supply chain logistics

Shops source their parts through different channels, and these logistics impact the final quote. A large repair centre might have a contract with a reputable wholesale supplier, allowing them to buy in bulk and ensure a steady stream of quality-tested parts. This scale can sometimes drive prices down, but it also means they are less likely to hunt for the 'cheapest' part on the market because their reputation depends on consistency.

Conversely, a smaller shop might source parts on an ad-hoc basis from different suppliers depending on who has stock that week. This approach can lead to fluctuating prices. Furthermore, some shops in specific regional hubs, such as those near Mayfield Phone Repair in Newcastle, may have different logistics costs compared to metropolitan centres. When parts have to be shipped to more remote locations or when a shop prioritises rapid turnaround times—requiring overnight shipping of components—those costs are factored into the service fee.

The value of the warranty

Not all warranties are created equal. When a shop quotes you, they are essentially including an insurance policy for their own work. A shop that offers a long-term warranty on the screen and the installation is betting that their parts are reliable and their technicians are skilled. If a shop quotes significantly less, look at the fine print. Are they offering a 30-day warranty, or a six-month guarantee? Are they covering the screen itself, or just the labour if it fails? A higher quote often includes the cost of supporting that repair long after you leave the counter, whereas a lower quote may offer very little protection if the screen develops a dead pixel or touch sensitivity issue a month later.

Ultimately, the price discrepancy usually comes down to a trade-off between speed, part quality, and the longevity of the repair. If you are preparing to trade in your phone, you might be comfortable with a mid-range part. If you plan to keep the device for another two years, investing in a higher-quality screen with a solid warranty will likely save you from having to pay for the same repair twice.
