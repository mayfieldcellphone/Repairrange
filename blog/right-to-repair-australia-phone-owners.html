Right to Repair Australia — What It Means for Phone Owners in 2026

By RepairRange · Independently researched pricing

Published 22 June 2026 · Written by RepairRange Editorial · 7 min read

Your phone screen cracks. You take it to an independent repair shop. They fix it for $120 instead of the manufacturer's $400 quote. Three months later, your speaker stops working — a completely unrelated issue. The manufacturer refuses the warranty claim because you used a third-party repairer. Sound familiar?

This scenario plays out across Australia every day. And it is exactly what the Right to Repair movement is fighting to change. Here is what Australian phone owners need to know in 2026.

What Is the Right to Repair?

The Right to Repair is a global consumer movement pushing for legislation that would require manufacturers to make spare parts, repair manuals, diagnostic software, and specialised tools available to independent repair shops and everyday consumers — not just their own authorised service networks.

The core argument is simple. You bought the phone. You own it. You should be able to choose who repairs it, or repair it yourself, without being punished by the manufacturer through voided warranties, software locks, or restricted parts access.

In Australia, the movement gained formal traction when the Productivity Commission published its inquiry into the Right to Repair in 2024, recommending legislative reforms modelled on the existing motor vehicle repair information sharing scheme. Consultations with industry and consumer groups are ongoing through 2026.

Your Existing Rights Under Australian Consumer Law

Even without specific Right to Repair legislation for electronics, Australian consumers already have strong protections under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL). Many people do not realise how powerful these protections actually are.

Key rights you already have

  • Third-party repair does not void your warranty. A manufacturer cannot refuse a warranty claim for an unrelated fault just because you used an independent repairer. They can only decline claims for damage directly caused by the third-party repair itself.
  • Consumer guarantees cannot be excluded. Products must be of acceptable quality, fit for purpose, and match their description. These guarantees apply regardless of any warranty document the manufacturer provides.
  • You are entitled to a remedy for major faults. If a product has a major failure, you can choose a refund or replacement — not just a repair. The manufacturer does not get to decide.
  • "Warranty void if removed" stickers are misleading. The ACCC has stated that these stickers may breach Australian Consumer Law if they imply that third-party repair voids the warranty.

What the Right to Repair Would Change

The proposed reforms go beyond existing consumer guarantees. If enacted, Right to Repair legislation for electronics would require manufacturers to provide independent repairers and consumers with access to genuine spare parts at fair prices, technical repair manuals and service documentation, diagnostic software and tools needed for calibration, and firmware updates that do not deliberately disable third-party components.

This last point is particularly relevant for phone owners. Apple's "non-genuine part" warnings, Samsung's software locks on replaced components, and Google's restricted calibration tools are all examples of manufacturer practices that Right to Repair legislation aims to address.

The "non-genuine part" problem

When you replace an iPhone screen or battery with a third-party part, iOS displays persistent warnings like "Unable to verify this iPhone has a genuine Apple display." These warnings do not affect functionality but are designed to discourage independent repair. Right to Repair advocates argue this is anti-competitive.

Why It Matters for Australian Phone Owners

Australia's phone repair market is worth an estimated $3.1 billion, driven by 23.6 million smartphone users. When manufacturers restrict repair access, prices go up, small businesses suffer, and working phones end up in landfill instead of being fixed.

Consider the numbers. An authorised Apple screen replacement for an iPhone 15 Pro Max costs around $579. An independent repairer using quality aftermarket parts charges $120–$180 for the same repair. A DIY kit from SelfRepairKit costs $99–$189 including all tools. The price difference is not about quality — it is about access and competition.

For regional Australians, the stakes are even higher. If your nearest authorised service centre is hours away, an independent local repairer or a DIY kit is not just cheaper — it is the only practical option.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does third-party repair void my phone warranty in Australia?

No. Under Australian Consumer Law, a manufacturer cannot void your entire warranty simply because you had the device repaired by an independent shop. They can only refuse warranty claims for damage directly caused by the third-party repair itself, not for unrelated issues.

Can I repair my own phone without voiding the warranty?

Under Australian Consumer Law, performing a DIY repair does not automatically void your warranty. However, the manufacturer is not liable for any damage you cause during the repair. If a separate issue arises that is unrelated to your repair, the manufacturer must still honour the consumer guarantee.

Are manufacturers required to provide spare parts in Australia?

Currently, there is no Australian law requiring electronics manufacturers to sell spare parts or share repair manuals with independent repairers or consumers. This is one of the key changes the Right to Repair movement is advocating for.
