Why Face ID stops working after a screen replacement

When you take your iPhone in for a screen replacement, the expectation is that the device will return to its original state. However, a common frustration for many users is discovering that Face ID no longer recognises their face once the repair is complete. This issue is not necessarily the result of a careless mistake; rather, it is a consequence of how Apple designs its hardware security systems and the extreme delicacy of the components involved.

The TrueDepth Camera System Complexity

Face ID relies on the TrueDepth camera system, which is a sophisticated array housed at the top of your screen. This system consists of a flood illuminator, a proximity sensor, an ambient light sensor, and the dot projector. These components are factory-calibrated to communicate directly with the phone’s logic board. They form a secure, hardware-locked chain; if any part of this chain is interrupted, damaged, or swapped during a repair, the biometric system effectively disables itself to prevent potential security breaches.

During a screen replacement, the technician must carefully remove this sensor assembly from the old screen and transplant it onto the new one. The cables connecting these sensors are thinner than a human hair and are highly susceptible to tearing. Even a microscopic nick or a slight tug during the disassembly process can permanently break the functionality of the biometric sensors. Because these components are serialised to the specific logic board of your phone, they cannot simply be replaced with a part from another unit.

Calibration and Serialisation

Modern iPhones use a process known as serialisation. When a genuine Apple part is replaced, the device often requires a software-based calibration through Apple’s proprietary tools to pair the new screen with the existing internal sensors. If this software handshake fails, or if a third-party screen is installed that lacks the necessary hardware integration, the device may flag the sensor array as unauthorised or faulty. In many instances, the system will actively disable Face ID to protect the integrity of the biometric data stored within the Secure Enclave.

This is why choosing where to have your device repaired matters. Skilled technicians who understand the architecture of these devices will approach the sensor transfer with immense caution, often using specialised heat mats to soften the adhesives that hold the sensors in place. The Risks of Third-Party Parts

Not all replacement screens are created equal. High-quality aftermarket screens often include the necessary mounting brackets and high-grade adhesive seals, but they do not possess the internal electronic components that Apple’s proprietary software expects to see. If the replacement screen is of lower quality, it may not even provide the correct physical alignment for the proximity sensor. If the sensor is even a fraction of a millimetre out of position, or if the glass over the sensors is slightly thicker or differently tinted, the infrared light will not pass through correctly, and Face ID will fail to authenticate.

Should You Proceed with the Repair?

Deciding whether to proceed with a screen replacement when Face ID is at risk involves weighing the utility of the feature against the necessity of a functioning screen. If the screen is shattered to the point of being unusable, you may have little choice. However, if the damage is minor, you should consider whether you are willing to live without biometric unlocking. Some users opt to continue using a passcode, while others decide that the cost and risk of damaging the TrueDepth system are too high.
