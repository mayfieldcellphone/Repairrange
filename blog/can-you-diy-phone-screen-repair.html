Can you fix your own phone screen? The honest answer.

By RepairRange · Independently researched pricing

Short answer: yes, on some phones. Long answer: it depends on your model, your risk tolerance, and whether you value your time at $0 or something higher. Here's the model-by-model reality from a technician who also sells DIY parts — so you know I'm not just trying to upsell you a shop repair.

The honest cost comparison

A DIY screen replacement saves you roughly 30-50% compared to paying a shop. But that savings comes with risk — if you crack the new screen during installation, damage the Face ID module, or tear a flex cable, that's your cost to bear. There's no warranty on your own labour.

Here's what the numbers actually look like for an iPhone 15 Pro Max screen replacement:

Shop repair: $329–$459 at an independent shop. Apple authorised: $359. Done in 60-90 minutes. 90-day warranty. Face ID guaranteed.

DIY repair: Screen kit from iFixit or eBay: $149–$249 for the part. Apple Self Service Repair: $199 for the genuine screen + $49 tool rental. Your time: 1.5–3 hours for a first-timer. No warranty on your work. Face ID risk if you make a mistake.

The real savings: $100–$200 — roughly the cost of 2-3 hours of your time. Whether that's "worth it" depends entirely on how you value your time and how comfortable you are with the risk.

Model-by-model DIY difficulty

Google Pixel 8/9 series — DIY difficulty: 4/10 (easiest flagship)

Google partners with iFixit to sell genuine Pixel parts with step-by-step video guides. The Pixel 8 Pro uses an optical fingerprint sensor (less problematic than Samsung's ultrasonic), the teardown is well-documented, and iFixit provides every tool you need in the kit. If you're going to DIY any flagship, this is the one to start with. Screen kits: $149–$229 AUD from iFixit.

iPhone 12–17 series — DIY difficulty: 6/10 (moderate)

Apple's Self Service Repair program provides genuine parts, official manuals, and tool rental ($49) for iPhone 12 and newer in Australia. The repair is well-documented but takes 1.5-2 hours for a first-timer. The critical risk is Face ID — you must carefully transfer the TrueDepth camera module from the old screen to the new one. If you damage or misalign it, Face ID is permanently lost and can only be re-paired using Apple's proprietary tools. Screen cost: $199–$379 via Apple Self Service. Aftermarket via iFixit: $129–$249.

Samsung Galaxy S21–S26 series — DIY difficulty: 7/10 (hard)

Samsung doesn't offer self-service repair in Australia. You'll source aftermarket screens from eBay, AliExpress, or specialist suppliers. The critical issue: the ultrasonic fingerprint sensor. Budget screens break it permanently. You must buy a sensor-compatible screen (labelled "with fingerprint" or "hard OLED"), which costs more. Samsung's adhesive is also stronger than iPhone's, making the opening process riskier — you need a heat gun and patience. See our Samsung fingerprint guide for the full explanation.

Samsung Galaxy Z Fold/Flip — DIY difficulty: 9/10 (don't)

Foldable screen replacement requires specialist tools, precise hinge alignment, and experience with UTG (ultra-thin glass). The inner screen on a Z Fold costs $400–$600+ for the part alone. One mistake and you've destroyed a $600 part. This is genuinely a professional-only repair. The shop cost ($599–$899) is worth every cent compared to the DIY risk.

iPhone SE 3 / iPhone 12 — DIY difficulty: 3/10 (great for beginners)

The iPhone SE 3 has an LCD screen (cheaper, simpler), Touch ID instead of Face ID (no pairing risk), and a compact body that's well-documented. The iPhone 12 has 5 years of aftermarket supply with cheap OLED parts. Both are excellent first-time DIY candidates. Screen kits: $59–$109 (SE 3) or $89–$149 (iPhone 12).

What tools you actually need

Every phone screen replacement requires the same basic toolkit:

Essential (included in most kits): Suction cup (to separate the screen from the frame), plastic pry tools/spudgers (to lever the screen without scratching), pentalobe screwdriver (for iPhone bottom screws — proprietary 5-point), Phillips/tri-wing screwdriver (for internal brackets), tweezers (for handling flex cables).

Usually needed but not always included: Heat gun or hair dryer on low (to soften adhesive — critical for Samsung), isopropyl alcohol (to clean adhesive residue), replacement adhesive strips (to reseal the phone after repair).

Nice to have: Anti-static mat, magnetic screw mat (keeps the tiny screws organised), iOpener heating bag (safer than a heat gun for beginners).

A complete kit from iFixit runs $20–$40 AUD. Most aftermarket screen sellers on eBay include a basic tool kit with the screen purchase.

The risks nobody tells you about

You will void your water resistance. Every phone screen replacement breaks the factory seal. Even if you reapply adhesive, the IP68 rating is effectively gone. Professional shops have the same limitation — no independent repair restores factory-level water resistance.

Warranty implications. Under Australian Consumer Law, DIY repair doesn't automatically void your manufacturer warranty. But if you cause damage during the repair, the manufacturer isn't responsible for that specific damage. If you're still under warranty, seriously consider letting the manufacturer do it — Apple's warranty screen replacements are free, and out-of-warranty pricing is competitive with DIY parts cost.

The "easy" repair that goes wrong. The most common DIY failure isn't the screen itself — it's tearing the thin flex cables that connect the battery, display, and cameras. These cables are fragile, barely visible, and positioned right where you're prying. One torn cable can turn a $149 screen kit into a $149 screen kit + $89 cable replacement at a shop that now has to fix your mistake.

Aftermarket screen quality varies wildly. A $79 screen from eBay and a $179 screen from iFixit look identical in the listing photos. The difference shows up in brightness (400 nits vs 1,200 nits), touch accuracy (phantom touches near edges), and longevity (6 months vs 2+ years). See our screen quality tiers guide to understand what you're buying.

When DIY makes sense vs when it doesn't

DIY makes sense when: The phone is out of warranty AND older than 18 months (mature parts market), you have basic mechanical aptitude (comfortable taking things apart), the model is rated 5/10 difficulty or lower (Pixel 8, iPhone SE, iPhone 12), and you value the learning experience.

Pay a shop when: The phone is under warranty (let the manufacturer do it for free), the model has biometric complications (Samsung S-series fingerprint, iPhone Face ID on Pro models), the phone is less than 12 months old (genuine parts may not be widely available yet), you need the phone back same-day (DIY takes 2-3 hours plus drying time for adhesive), or the phone has sentimental/business-critical data and you can't afford the risk.

The middle ground: Apple Self Service Repair gives you genuine parts with official instructions but at your own pace. This is the best option for confident DIYers who want quality parts without the aftermarket lottery.

The bottom line

Can you fix your own phone screen? Technically, yes — for most models. Should you? That depends on the model, your skill level, and whether saving $100–$200 is worth 2-3 hours of your time plus the risk of making things worse.
