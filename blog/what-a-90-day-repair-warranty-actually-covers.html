What a 90-Day Repair Warranty Actually Covers: A Practical Guide

When you leave a repair shop with your device in hand, the transaction feels complete. However, the true test of a repair often happens in the weeks that follow. Most reputable repairers offer a 90-day warranty, but this terminology is frequently misunderstood. It is not an insurance policy against accidents or a guarantee that your device will last forever. Instead, it is a narrow safety net designed specifically to cover the workmanship and the individual parts installed during that singular repair event.

Defining Workmanship and Part Failure

The core of a 90-day warranty rests on two pillars: the quality of the installation and the integrity of the component used. If a technician replaces a charging port, the warranty covers the work involved in soldering or connecting that component, as well as the functionality of the new part itself. If the charging port stops working because the solder joint failed or the part itself was defective from the manufacturer, the repairer is responsible for rectifying the issue at no additional labour cost.

However, this coverage is strictly limited to the scope of that specific job. If you had a screen replaced and the charging port fails two weeks later, the warranty on the screen repair does not extend to the charging port. Each repair is a distinct technical event with its own documentation. The Critical Distinction Between Warranty and Damage

The most common point of friction between customers and repairers is the definition of a fault. A warranty is designed to address failures that arise from the repair process itself, not events that happen to the device after it has left the shop. If you drop your phone and the screen cracks again, this is classified as accidental damage, not a warranty claim. Similarly, liquid ingress, pressure damage, or software updates that cause system glitches are generally outside the scope of a standard 90-day repair warranty.

Technical workshops are often able to distinguish between a faulty screen digitiser—which might show lines or ghost touching without external force—and a screen that has been impacted by an object. Reputable shops keep records of the device's condition at the point of handover. Why Documentation Matters

Your warranty is only as useful as the paperwork you hold. When a repair is completed, you should receive an invoice that clearly lists the parts used, the date of service, and the warranty terms. This document is your proof of purchase and your reference point should something go wrong. Without a clear trail of what was repaired, proving that a specific fault relates to a previous service is difficult. Most shops will require this documentation before they even open the device for a warranty inspection.

If you are unsure about whether to proceed with a repair or simply replace an older device, it helps to compare the longevity of current repairs against the total value of your hardware. Evaluating your options helps prevent the sunk-cost fallacy where you spend more on recurring repairs than the device is actually worth.

The Limits of Coverage

It is important to recognise that a 90-day warranty does not cover the entire device. If you have a battery replaced under warranty, the shop is not guaranteeing that the motherboard, the camera module, or the speakers will remain operational. The warranty is tied strictly to the work performed. If a device has inherent design flaws or age-related degradation in other parts, the repairer cannot be held responsible for those unrelated failures.

Furthermore, third-party repairs can sometimes affect the manufacturer's original warranty. While the Australian Consumer Law provides protections that exist alongside repairer warranties, it is helpful to be clear about who is responsible for what. Always ask the repairer to explain their specific warranty policy before they begin, and clarify if there are any specific conditions—such as voiding the warranty if the device is opened by another party—that you should be aware of.

Managing your expectations regarding repairs means viewing the 90-day period as a window to ensure the job was done correctly. By keeping your receipts, understanding the difference between a defect and accidental damage, and knowing exactly which components are covered, you can make informed decisions about your technology and ensure you get the most out of your repair budget.
