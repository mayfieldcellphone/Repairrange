INCELL vs OLED vs OEM: Will My Replacement Screen Actually Work Fine?

A $35 INCELL and a $250 OEM aren't the same product with different markups. They're fundamentally different technologies — different materials, different thickness, different fit, different lifespan. This is everything the repair shop doesn't tell you.

The honest answer

Yes, all three screen types work. Your phone will make calls, run apps, and display content with any tier. But the difference between a $35 INCELL and a $150 OLED is not just visual — it's physical fit, durability, battery life, and whether your screen survives the next drop. If your phone originally came with an OLED screen (iPhone X and newer, all Samsung S-series), putting in an INCELL is a technology downgrade, not just a quality tier.

The three technologies explained

Before comparing tiers, you need to understand that INCELL and OLED are fundamentally different technologies — like replacing a diesel engine with petrol. Both move the car, but the engineering is completely different.

INCELL LCD — Budget tier

$22–$99 AUD · LCD technology

What it is: An LCD panel with the touch digitiser layer integrated directly into the LCD (that's what "in-cell" means). It uses a backlight behind the entire panel to illuminate pixels — the backlight is always on, even when displaying black.

Physical design: The backlight layer adds thickness. An INCELL assembly is measurably thicker than the original OLED screen on modern phones. This extra thickness means the glass sits higher than the phone's metal frame, creating visible raised edges that protrude beyond the housing.

Best for: Phones that originally shipped with LCD screens — iPhone 7, 8, XR, 11, SE. On these models, INCELL matches the original technology. On phones that came with OLED (iPhone X+, Samsung S-series), INCELL is a downgrade.

Aftermarket OLED — Best value ⭐

$52–$179 AUD · OLED technology

What it is: A brand-new OLED panel made by a third-party manufacturer (not Apple or Samsung's factory, but using the same OLED technology). Each pixel produces its own light — no backlight needed. Black pixels are literally turned off.

Two sub-types: Soft OLED uses a flexible plastic substrate (same as the original) — thinner, more drop-resistant, sits flush in the frame. Hard OLED uses a rigid glass substrate — slightly cheaper, slightly thicker, slightly more brittle. Soft OLED is what we recommend.

Best for: Any phone that originally came with an OLED screen. Restores the original technology at 40–60% of OEM price. Our best seller — 80% of SelfRepairKit customers choose this tier.

OEM Original — Premium

$65–$349 AUD · Factory original

What it is: A brand-new, factory-sealed original screen — identical to the part inside your phone right now. Made on the same production line as Apple or Samsung's own screens. Full serialisation, full calibration, full feature compatibility.

Best for: People who want zero compromise. Full True Tone, full Battery Health compatibility (when paired), factory-spec colour accuracy. Also the best choice if you plan to sell the phone — OEM screens preserve resale value.

Why INCELL screens have thick edges that bulge out

This is the #1 complaint about budget screen replacements, and it's caused by physics, not poor manufacturing.

OLED screens have no backlight. Each pixel is an individual organic LED that produces its own light. The display assembly is extremely thin — typically 1.0–1.4mm. This is why your original iPhone or Samsung screen sits perfectly flush with the metal frame. Apple and Samsung designed the phone's frame depth around the thickness of an OLED panel.

INCELL screens have a backlight layer. Behind the LCD panel sits a sheet of LEDs (the backlight) that illuminates the entire screen uniformly. This adds approximately 0.3–0.5mm to the total assembly thickness. That doesn't sound like much — but when the phone's frame was designed to hold a 1.2mm OLED panel and you're inserting a 1.6mm INCELL panel, the extra 0.4mm pushes the glass surface above the frame edge.

This raised edge creates two problems:

  1. It looks wrong. You can see and feel the screen protruding above the frame. A case partially hides this, but running your finger along the edge reveals the step. On phones with curved-edge frames (iPhone 12+), it's more noticeable.
  2. It breaks easier. When you drop an OLED phone, the metal frame hits the ground first and absorbs the impact — the screen is recessed and protected. When you drop an INCELL phone, the raised glass edge is the first contact point. Direct glass-to-ground impact. This is why INCELL screens break more frequently than OLED — it's not that the glass is weaker (it's often the same glass), it's that the glass takes the impact instead of the frame.

The false economy: An INCELL screen costs $35–$99. If it breaks within 6 months because of the raised-edge problem, you'll spend another $35–$99 plus your time to replace it again. The OLED at $80–$179 often costs less over a year because it sits flush and survives drops that would crack an INCELL.

The battery drain problem nobody mentions

INCELL screens use an always-on backlight. Every pixel — white, black, grey — gets the same backlight intensity. When you're viewing a dark app, dark mode, or a night scene in a video, the backlight is still running at full power behind those "black" pixels.

OLED screens turn off black pixels completely. No light, no power draw. Studies show OLED uses up to 60% less battery than LCD when displaying dark content. If you use dark mode (most people do in 2026), an OLED screen directly improves your battery life. An INCELL screen worsens it compared to the original OLED.

So replacing your OLED screen with an INCELL doesn't just change how it looks — it changes how fast your battery drains. If you replaced the screen because the battery was struggling, an INCELL screen makes the battery problem worse.

When INCELL is actually fine

INCELL isn't always the wrong choice. There are situations where it's perfectly appropriate:

  • Phones that originally had LCD screens — iPhone 7, 8, XR, 11, SE. These shipped with LCD technology, so an INCELL replacement matches the original. No thickness issue, no feature loss, no downgrade.
  • Temporary fix before selling — if you're selling the phone in 2–4 weeks and just need a working screen for the buyer to test, INCELL saves money on a phone you won't keep.
  • Budget-first on phones worth under $200 — if the phone's resale value is low, spending $150 on an OLED screen doesn't make economic sense.
  • Samsung Galaxy A-series (A15, A25) — the budget A-series originally ships with LCD/AMOLED, and INCELL is a reasonable match.

The simple rule: If your phone originally came with OLED → replace with OLED or OEM. If it originally came with LCD → INCELL is fine. Don't downgrade from OLED to LCD. You'll notice the difference every time you use the phone.

Samsung-specific: The fingerprint sensor problem

Samsung Galaxy S-series phones (S21 and newer) have an ultrasonic fingerprint sensor embedded inside the OLED display. This sensor requires the screen to be OLED — it physically cannot work through an LCD panel.

If you put an INCELL screen on a Galaxy S24:

  • Fingerprint unlock is permanently disabled
  • No warning, no error — the option simply disappears from settings
  • The only fix is replacing the screen again with OLED or OEM

This means an INCELL screen on a Samsung S-series phone isn't just a visual downgrade — it removes a core security feature. Full Samsung fingerprint guide →

How to tell what tier you're getting

Some suppliers mislabel screens. A screen advertised as "OLED" might actually be INCELL. Here's how to verify before and after installation:

Before installation

  • Check thickness — hold the screen edge-on. OLED is noticeably thinner. If it looks chunky, it's INCELL.
  • Check flexibility — soft OLED panels have a slight flex when gently bent. INCELL and hard OLED are rigid. (Don't bend aggressively.)
  • Check the flex cable label — look for model numbers. LTPS in the label = LCD/INCELL. AMOLED in the label = genuine OLED.
  • Check the price — if an "OLED" screen costs $35, it's almost certainly INCELL. Genuine OLED panels cost more to manufacture.

After installation

  • Display a pure black image — on OLED, the screen looks completely off. On INCELL, you'll see a faint grey glow from the backlight (especially visible in a dark room).
  • Check the edges — run your finger along the screen-to-frame junction. OLED sits flush. INCELL protrudes slightly.
  • Check True Tone — Settings → Display → True Tone. If the toggle exists and works, it's OLED or OEM. If it's missing, it's INCELL.
  • Check 120Hz — on Pro models, Settings → Display → ProMotion. If it's locked to 60Hz, you have INCELL.

Our honest recommendation

Choose INCELL if:

Your phone originally had LCD (iPhone 7/8/XR/11/SE, Samsung A15/A25). Temporary fix before selling. Budget is the absolute priority and you understand the trade-offs.

Choose OLED if: ⭐ (recommended for most people)

Your phone originally had OLED. You want flush-fit, better battery, and drop protection. Samsung S-series (preserves fingerprint). Best value per dollar.

Choose OEM if:

You want factory-identical. Planning to resell. Need full True Tone + Battery Health pairing. Want zero compromise and zero warnings.
